Solar Drift

A solo space survival about finding one good corner of a broken system to call home.

The world

The solar system is broken. Not from war — from accumulation. Technologies turned against their builders not out of malice or intent, but because cascading failures compounded until they couldn't be undone. There was no villain. There was no final battle. It just happened.

The rogue machines still run. They do what they were built to do, in a world that no longer makes sense for it. They aren't enemies. They're the wreckage.

But life kept going anyway. Generations of adapted Earth-origin organisms have quietly colonized the ruins. Caves on Mars bloom with pale plant life. Balloon-vines drift through the cloud layers of Venus. The solar system didn't mourn — it moved on, and built something strange and new in the silence.

The player

You woke up. The cryo system had been running so long that whoever you were before is effectively gone — memories dissolved, identity reset. You are, functionally, a new person born into an aftermath you have no context for.

You are silent. Not because you can't speak, but because you have no words yet for what you're experiencing. The player fills that silence.

Somewhere in the ruins, there are logs. Messages. A face that was yours. A life you don't remember living. You have no obligation to that person — but you might feel one anyway.

The goal is simple, and enormous: find somewhere to rebuild. Not save the system. Not avenge anything. Just find one place worth staying.

The ship

The ship is your constant. The only thing that was there before you woke up, and the only thing that will be there for all of it. It has no voice. It doesn't reassure you. It just holds together — or doesn't — based on what you do.

The ship accumulates memory physically. That dent in the left hull panel is from a debris field three systems back. The jury-rigged sensor array is from the time a swarm caught you off guard. The ship becomes a diary of everything you've survived together, written in repairs and improvised fixes.

Complexity is abstracted. The engine is one block to manage, not fifty. The controls are simple enough to never frustrate. But the systems are real enough to feel like yours — like a tool you've used so long it becomes an extension of your hand.

Threats & awareness

The world doesn't wait for you. Rogue tech swarms move. Safe spaces shift. Solar storms build on the horizon. Debris from previous conflicts drifts through shipping lanes that no longer exist.

Awareness splits into two layers. A passive proximity radar catches what's already close — your safety net, your guarantee of fairness. But surviving well means looking ahead: pointing sensors toward where you expect trouble, building early warning systems, setting defensive traps. Never so many that it becomes tower defense. Preparation is a ritual, not a profession.

The information lives on the ship's screens and consoles, not floating in front of your face. You read the world the way you'd read instruments. You learn what the silence sounds like before a swarm moves.

Other survivors

They exist. Rare enough that finding one feels like an event. They're not companions in the traditional sense — more like lighthouses. People who found somewhere worth protecting, and occasionally decide you're worth trusting with it.

When your camp gets hit and the ship is barely holding and someone guides you to a safe spot they'd been keeping secret — that only works emotionally because it's rare. Because they chose you.

There are also the ones who didn't make it. Messages and logs frozen mid-thought in the ruins of dead stations. You piece together what happened through fragments left by people trying to survive the same thing you are.

The world as it is now

Scale is light FTL — barely above the speed of light. This stays solar-system bound. It's not a galaxy-spanning epic. It's intimate. The smallness is the point.

Each location is distinct because life adapted differently in each place. Mars is not Venus is not the asteroid belt. The beauty isn't decoration — sometimes you stop and look for a moment before moving on. Not everything needs a mechanic.

You're not looking at places that survived. You're looking at places that healed without you. That can be hopeful. Sometimes it's devastating.

The ending — or the beginning of after

The colony is the anchor. The moment something gets built that has a heartbeat again — that's the threshold. But it's not an ending. The ship is still outside, waiting.

Some players will build the colony and feel complete. Some will chase every log and survivor and fragment of a prior life before settling. Some will never fully settle — just keep going, which is its own kind of answer.

No forced credits. No win screen. Just a moment where you look at what's been built, and the ship waiting outside, and you decide what the story means.
